About

The Regenerative Atlas is a landscape of regenerative research — submitted and edited by the people who use it, and curated by Malina Dabrowska. It is a collective knowledge exchange: a place to find the connections and kinship between themes, reports, papers and current thinking across regenerative practice, to explore them as constellations, and to add to the library yourself.

A landscape, not a list

Regenerative practice is being written in a hundred places at once — a standard here, a case study there, a paper that only makes sense next to a report nobody has put beside it yet. A reading list flattens all of that into an order somebody chose. A map does not: it shows what sits near what, and lets you arrive at a subject from whichever direction you came.

So the Atlas is drawn rather than listed. Every tag is a shape whose size is the research filed under it; pieces that share a vocabulary are drawn together into islands; the lines between them are kinship, which is to say that two pieces are filed in enough of the same places to be worth reading together. Nothing here is ranked. The map shows what is related, not what is important — that judgement stays with you.

Added by the people who use it

Anyone can add a source, and nobody owns their entries. Adding one takes a link: the Atlas reads what it can from the page, suggests tags from a curated vocabulary, and a person decides where it sits. That decision is the contribution — where a piece is filed is what makes the map mean anything, and it is not a judgement a machine should be making on its own.

Entries can be corrected and re-filed as the field moves. The vocabulary grows the same way: it holds the words the practice actually uses, with the many written forms of a subject folded into one, so that LCA, life cycle analysis and life-cycle assessment all land in the same place.

Curated

The Atlas is curated by Malina Dabrowska, a designer and strategist exploring regenerative practice within the built environment. Curation here means tending rather than gatekeeping: keeping the vocabulary coherent, merging what is the same thing under two names, and making sure the shape of the map still answers to the shape of the field.

Yours to leave with

The library is open. Every record can be taken away — the reading under any one theme as plain text, or the whole library as JSON or BibTeX — and the site itself is open source, so it can be forked, self-hosted, or simply read. Bibliographic records are shared under CC0; the linked works remain with their authors.
